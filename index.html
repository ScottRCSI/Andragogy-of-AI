The Andragogy of AI

Your expertise is the point. AI just extends its reach.

A theoretical framework for the human side of enterprise AI adoption, built on how adults actually learn rather than how institutions prefer to teach.

The Problem

Something is not working. And it never has.

Every major technology in history has produced the same lag between capability and adoption, and the institutional response has always been the same mistake: more instruction, clearer mandates, sharper efficiency arguments.

The technology arrives. The human adaptation lags. Each time, organizations respond with compliance design aimed at adults who learn through autonomy.

The Framework

Six principles of how adults learn, remapped for AI

Malcolm Knowles spent a career articulating how adults learn differently from how children are taught. Each assumption becomes a design principle for adoption.

01

Self-concept

Adults are self-directing and resist being positioned as dependent learners.
For AI adoption

Mandated training undermines autonomy. Opt-in exploration preserves self-direction and produces deeper, more durable engagement.

02

Experience

Prior experience is the filter and resource through which new learning passes.
For AI adoption

Framing AI as an extension of existing expertise produces faster adoption than framing it as a replacement.

03

Readiness

Adults learn when they face a real problem, not when instruction is scheduled.
For AI adoption

Contextual, just-in-time interventions hit the teachable moment. Scheduled awareness campaigns miss it.

04

Orientation

Adults are problem-centered, not subject-centered. Learning must apply now.
For AI adoption

Programs built around real work problems produce higher engagement than general capability training.

05

Motivation

Intrinsic drivers (mastery, growth, contribution) outperform external pressure.
For AI adoption

Efficiency narratives appeal to organizational metrics. Capability-expansion framing aligns with what actually moves people.

06

Need to know

Adults need to understand why before they invest in learning something.
For AI adoption

"Why should I use this?" must be answered in terms of individual benefit, not organizational efficiency or competitive necessity.

The Identity Subtext Audit

Every message about AI sends an implicit signal about the value of human expertise. The frame, independent of the facts, shapes the response. Same tool. Same time saved. The only thing that changes is the framing.

Amplification framing

Why Resistance Happens

Three identity threats, three different responses

Resistance is not one phenomenon. Two people in the same training session can be experiencing entirely different threats. Each is legitimate, and each needs its own design response.

Expertise threat

"Do years of hard-won judgment still matter if AI approximates it in seconds?"
What is happening

Knowledge that took a career to build appears devalued. The fear is not about the tool. It is about whether the investment still counts.

What the program needs

Role-anchored demonstrations that make visible what AI cannot do without the practitioner's judgment. Generic demos trigger threat; force-multiplier framing activates curiosity.

details

Status threat

"What happens to my standing when specialized knowledge is suddenly everywhere?"
What is happening

Democratized knowledge disrupts the peer hierarchies experienced professionals spent careers building.

What the program needs

Create new peer-visible markers of sophisticated use. Recognize depth of judgment and quality of AI direction, not speed or volume.

details

Autonomy threat

"If the workflow is mandated, where does my own agency go?"
What is happening

Mandated workflows remove expressive agency. Underneath sits a longer fear: that judgment will atrophy as reliance grows.

What the program needs

Design adoption as opt-in experimentation. Where use is unavoidable, preserve discretion inside it. Autonomy threat dissolves when adoption feels like expansion, not subtraction.

What Makes This Different

Why the existing frameworks fall short

Andragogy, Self-Determination Theory, and identity-threat research all predate generative AI. Two of its features change the stakes, and the existing literature has not fully mapped either.

Problem one

The artifact problem

Generative AI produces the visible products of expertise: documents, analyses, code, creative work. Prior productivity tools automated tasks. This approximates the artifacts of professional judgment itself, which is qualitatively different.

Problem two

The visibility problem

Workers can compare their own output to AI output in real time, in a way earlier tools never allowed. That comparison intensifies both expertise threat and autonomy threat beyond what existing identity research has accounted for.

These do not argue against applying andragogy to AI. They argue for it more urgently. The identity stakes are higher, and the need for expert-amplification framing is more acute.

Research Status

A framework built to be tested

The Andragogy of AI is a theoretical contribution that calls for empirical validation. Its propositions are testable, and testing them is the next step. This is a beginning, built to be challenged. Being proven partly wrong is the point.

  • H1Andragogically designed programs produce higher sustained use than efficiency-framed programs, controlling for tool quality.
  • H2Psychological safety mediates the link between program design and willingness to experiment.
  • H3Identity-threat type moderates the relationship between adoption messaging and adoption intention.
  • H4Peer social proof influences adoption behavior more strongly than leadership endorsement.
